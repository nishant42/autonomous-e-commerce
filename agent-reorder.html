Twenty white, twenty black, and the reorder that destroys the year

The case put to me was this. We sold twenty white towels and twenty black towels. At reorder time we bought twice as much white and no black. Why would anyone do that, and what intelligence stops it? The answer is not a better forecasting model. It is that units sold is not demand, and almost every inventory decision in small retail is made on the wrong number.

The short version
  • A stockout makes a product look dead. Black sold out on day 22 and recorded zero sales for 68 days. On a sales report that reads as "nobody wants black".
  • Being always available makes a weak product look steady. White was in stock all 90 days and discounted, so it dribbled out twenty units and looked like the reliable seller.
  • The same twenty units mean four times the demand. Corrected for availability, black ran at 0.91 units/day against white's 0.22. Black's true 90-day demand was about 80 units, not 20.
  • Part of white's sales were black's. Customers who wanted black and found it gone bought white. The naive reading does not just understate black, it overstates white.
  • The fix is a data decision, not a model. Nobody stores historical stock availability, so the evidence is destroyed daily. An hourly inventory snapshot is the first thing built, before any agent.
This is implemented, not just described

The engine in engine/ runs these rules on 90 days of seeded history with no dependencies. cd engine && python3 run.py ui opens an operator console at 127.0.0.1:8787 where a cycle can be run and watched job by job, proposals approved or rejected, and every number clicked back to the query that produced it. python3 run.py demo prints the same decisions in a terminal, and this static snapshot is what run.py console generates — the same decisions, read back out of the database, with every intermediate number, the gateway's verdict on each proposal and the full audit trail.

The cost of the wrong reorder

The human decision bought 40 white at €7 cost (€280 of working capital in a product with 18% returns that only moves on discount, roughly 15 months of cover) and zero black. On the agent's reading, the next quarter loses around €880 of contribution margin in unserved black demand, plus the markdown needed to clear the white. Across a portfolio of ten products and a few hundred variants, this one class of error is the difference between a business that compounds and one that is permanently out of cash with a full warehouse.

Why this happens, including with inventory software

CauseMechanismWhat the agent does differently
Censored observationYou can only observe sales up to what you had. Zero stock produces zero sales, which is indistinguishable from zero demand in the dataFits demand only on days the variant was actually buyable, using availability as an exposure offset
Recency bias in the report"Last 60 days" windows punish the item that stocked out early and reward the item that lingeredWindows are measured in available days, never calendar days
Substitution counted as successDemand displaced from the out-of-stock variant inflates the in-stock one, so the system doubles down on the weaker productEstimates a substitution share for periods when a sibling variant was out of stock and removes it from the baseline
Discounted volume treated as demandUnits moved at −30% are counted the same as full-price unitsBaseline is price-adjusted; promotional days are flagged features, and the forecast is for the price we intend to charge
Returns never joinedGross units drive the buy; the returns rate lives in a support dashboardForecasts net units and uses margin after returns as the ordering objective
Off-the-shelf tools inherit the same blind spotMost Shopify replenishment apps forecast from the sales series and, at best, let a human tick "ignore stockout period". They cannot reconstruct what they never recordedWe record availability hourly from day one, which is what makes the reconstruction possible at all. This is the honest reason to build the decision layer rather than buy it.

The method, step by step

Figure D2.1: Steps C, D and E are the ones that would have saved the towels, and all three depend on data that must be captured before it is needed.

StepTechniqueWhy not something simpler
Reconstruct demandNegative-binomial GLM with log(available_hours) as offset; Poisson where dispersion is low. Fitted per style with variant effects, so thin variants are not fitted aloneRate-of-sale per available day already recovers most of the value and is the fallback. The GLM adds proper handling of partial-day availability, overdispersion and seasonality, and gives intervals rather than a point
SubstitutionEstimate the displaced share from sibling-out-of-stock windows: compare the in-stock variant's rate during sibling-OOS periods against its own rate when all siblings were availableIgnoring it systematically rewards whichever variant happened to be available. That is precisely the towel failure
Unmet-demand evidenceBack-in-stock sign-ups, product views while unbuyable, failed add-to-cart, site-search terms. Used as a lower bound and a cross-check, never as the primary estimateThese signals are biased but directionally strong, and they are free. Where they contradict the model by more than 50%, the agent raises the variant for human review rather than guessing
Price adjustmentLog-log elasticity from the SKU's own promotional history; category prior when there is noneForecasting at a blended historic price then buying for full-price selling is how overstock is created
HierarchyBottom-up forecast with MinT-style reconciliation to style and category totalsA new colour in an established style has no history of its own but inherits a reliable share curve
ValidationRolling-origin backtest, 8 folds; metrics are weighted MAPE, bias, and a service-and-stock simulation (would this policy have stocked out, and with how much capital tied up)Forecast accuracy alone is a vanity metric. The agent is scored on the decisions the forecast caused

The data that must exist, and the job that creates it

The one irreversible mistake

Availability history cannot be reconstructed later. If the hourly snapshot is not running from day one, the towel analysis is impossible a quarter from now — the evidence is simply gone. This is why the snapshot job is the first thing in the build order, ahead of every agent.

From demand to quantity: the service level is an economic choice

A blanket "95% service level" is a guess dressed as a policy. The agent derives it per SKU from the newsvendor critical ratio, so the service level on a high-margin, long-shelf-life item is genuinely different from a thin-margin perishable.

critical_ratio = Cu / (Cu + Co)

  Cu (cost of understocking) = contribution margin lost per unit of unmet demand
                               + an estimated share of lost customer lifetime value
  Co (cost of overstocking)  = holding cost over the expected sell-through time
                               + expected markdown to clear
                               + obsolescence (for perishables: full write-off after shelf_life_days)

service_level = critical_ratio            -> the quantile of the demand distribution to cover
order_up_to   = quantile(demand over lead_time + review_period, service_level)
order_qty     = max(0, order_up_to - on_hand - on_order - in_transit)
              -> rounded up to case pack, checked against MOQ and price breaks
              -> capped by cover_cap_weeks, open_to_buy and the cash runway floor

What the buyer actually receives

One proposal per supplier per cycle: the lines, the quantities, the cover after arrival, the capital committed, and for every quantity a one-line reason of the form "black towel: 0.91/day over 22 available days, 41 back-in-stock sign-ups, substitution-corrected demand 80 ±14, 90% service level, 60 units at case pack 12, 9.9 weeks cover" — each number clickable back to the query that produced it. That is the difference between an autonomous buyer and a black box.
